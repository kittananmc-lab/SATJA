SATJA: A Global Resource-Referenced Monetary and Exchange Framework
Concept Proposal, System Architecture, Economic Principles, Risks, and Research Agenda
Version: 1.2
Date: 19 November 2026
Proposed by: Kittanan Kaewmai, Thailand
Project: SATJA (SJT)
Publication status: Open concept proposal for public study, discussion, research, and further development. This document does not describe an operational currency or a fully validated financial system.
Abstract
SATJA is a proposed global monetary and exchange framework designed to use measurable real-world resources as references for a shared unit of account.
The central idea is that international exchange should not depend exclusively on the monetary value of any single national currency. Instead, a common reference unit could be calculated using transparent information about a diversified range of real resources, goods, productive assets, and services.
Potential reference categories include agricultural products, livestock, eggs, rice, land, water, energy, industrial materials, machinery, technology, and other measurable economic resources.
SATJA proposes a framework in which resource information is recorded, evaluated, updated, and independently verified according to transparent standards. Buyers and sellers would remain free to negotiate prices rather than being forced to accept a single global price.
The proposed framework may eventually support functions commonly found in modern financial systems, including deposits, withdrawals, transfers, lending, interest, purchases, sales, and investment. However, each function would require appropriate legal, economic, technical, and institutional safeguards.
This paper presents the concept, proposed architecture, valuation principles, governance requirements, risks, and a research roadmap. It invites researchers, economists, developers, agricultural producers, businesses, governments, and the international community to examine and improve the proposal.
Keywords: SATJA, SJT, resource-referenced money, global monetary framework, real-world assets, monetary economics, international exchange, blockchain, financial transparency.
1. Introduction
Modern economic activity relies heavily on money as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value.
National currencies are essential to contemporary economies. However, international transactions can be affected by exchange-rate movements, inflation, monetary policy differences, unequal purchasing power, and the economic conditions of individual countries.
A product with similar physical characteristics may have substantially different prices across countries. Exchange rates and differences in local purchasing power can make international comparisons difficult.
SATJA is proposed as an alternative framework for studying how a shared unit of account could be referenced to real economic resources rather than being defined solely through the monetary policy of one country.
The objective is not necessarily to replace existing currencies. The initial objective is to establish a transparent common reference for measuring and comparing economic value across borders.
SATJA seeks to answer a fundamental question:
Can international economic exchange become more transparent and comparable when a shared monetary reference is connected to measurable real-world resources?
2. Vision and Objectives
The long-term vision of SATJA is a global framework for fairer, more transparent, and more understandable economic exchange.
Its objectives include:
Establishing a shared unit of account that is not exclusively tied to one national currency.
Using real-world resources as measurable references for economic value.
Developing transparent methods for collecting and updating resource data.
Supporting voluntary price negotiation between buyers and sellers.
Reducing dependence on any single country's currency as the only international reference.
Supporting financial services through a carefully governed architecture.
Encouraging international cooperation and independent research.
Making the framework open to public examination, criticism, and improvement.
SATJA should be developed through evidence and independent evaluation rather than relying on claims of superiority over existing monetary systems.
3. Core Principles
3.1 Real-World Resource References
The proposed unit of account should be referenced to a diversified set of observable economic resources.
Examples include:
Agricultural products.
Rice and other staple foods.
Eggs and livestock.
Meat and dairy products.
Land and property rights.
Water resources.
Electricity and other energy resources.
Industrial materials.
Machinery and equipment.
Technology and productive assets.
Services and other measurable economic activities.
Not every resource must be included in the reference system. Each category must meet defined requirements for data quality, measurability, availability, and verification.
3.2 Continuous Data Updating
Resource information should be updated as market conditions and physical circumstances change.
For example, egg prices may change daily according to production, supply, demand, quality, and location.
Land is a different category. Its physical existence may remain stable over long periods, while its market value can change because of location, ownership rights, infrastructure, legal restrictions, and market conditions.
Therefore, SATJA should not apply one depreciation rule to every resource. Different resources require different measurement and updating methods.
3.3 Transparency
The rules governing resource registration, valuation, data updates, and financial operations should be publicly documented.
Users should be able to understand how reference values are calculated and how changes are made.
3.4 Voluntary Exchange
SATJA should not force all sellers to accept an identical price.
A shared unit of account provides a common way to express value. Actual transaction prices may still differ according to quality, location, availability, transportation costs, supply, demand, and negotiated agreements.
3.5 Independent Verification
Resource information should be supported by evidence and independent verification wherever appropriate.
A digital record alone cannot prove that a physical asset exists, belongs to a particular person, or is available for delivery.
3.6 Equal Access and Fair Participation
The framework should aim to provide understandable and accessible rules for individuals, businesses, producers, and institutions across different countries.

4.1 Preventing Duplicate Registration
A resource used as collateral or backing for a financial obligation must not be represented as freely available in multiple conflicting claims.
The system should include controls to detect:
Duplicate asset registrations.
Multiple conflicting ownership claims.
Double pledging.
Previously transferred assets.
Expired or invalid documentation.
Disputed legal rights.
4.2 Different Resources Require Different Evidence
An egg inventory, a parcel of land, a livestock herd, and a power-generation facility cannot be verified in the same way.
For example:
Eggs may require quantity counts, grading, production dates, and inventory inspection.
Livestock may require animal identification, health records, and physical inspection.
Land may require official title documents, boundary verification, and legal checks.
Energy may require metering data and delivery records.
Machinery may require serial numbers, ownership documents, condition reports, and inspection.
The registry should define category-specific verification standards.
5. The SATJA Unit of Account and Valuation Framework
SATJA proposes SJT as a shared reference unit for expressing and comparing economic value.
The exact definition of one SJT has not yet been finalized. It must be established through research, testing, and transparent public consultation.
5.1 Reference Basket
A possible approach is to create a diversified reference basket of resources.
Each resource category would have:
A clearly defined specification.
A measurable unit.
A documented price source.
A geographic coverage method.
A data update frequency.
A quality adjustment method.
A weighting methodology.
For example, an illustrative reference basket might include agricultural products, energy, industrial materials, and other economically significant resources.
5.2 Illustrative Index Formula
A possible research model is:
[ I_t = 100 \times \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i R_{i,t} ]
Where:
(I_t) = reference index at time (t).
(w_i) = assigned weight for resource category (i).
(R_{i,t}) = normalized value of resource category (i) at time (t).
(n) = number of resource categories.
The weights must satisfy:
[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i = 1 ]
This formula is illustrative, not a finalized monetary standard.
A real implementation would require careful treatment of price volatility, missing observations, regional differences, quality changes, market concentration, and manipulation risks.
5.3 Quality and Comparability
A kilogram of one agricultural product is not necessarily equivalent to a kilogram of another.
Even within the same category, quality grades and specifications may differ.
Therefore, resource prices should be compared only after appropriate standardization.
5.4 Regional Price Differences
Resource prices differ between countries and regions.
SATJA should investigate whether its reference index should use:
International benchmark prices.
Regional market prices.
Weighted national observations.
Purchasing-power adjustments.
A combination of these approaches.
The methodology must be published and open to independent review.
5.5 Reference Value Is Not Automatically Redemption Value
A critical distinction must be maintained between:
A resource used to calculate a reference index.
An asset legally owned by an institution.
An asset pledged as collateral.
An asset held in reserve.
A resource that can actually be delivered or redeemed.
These concepts are not interchangeable.
A resource-referenced unit does not automatically guarantee that every SJT can be exchanged for a specific quantity of physical resources.
Any redemption promise would require enforceable legal rights, sufficient reserves, operational arrangements, and independent verification.
6. Monetary and Financial Architecture
SATJA is intended to explore a framework that could eventually support familiar financial functions.
These functions should be developed as separate modules with appropriate legal and financial safeguards.
6.1 Unit of Account
SJT would provide a shared way to express prices, values, obligations, and economic comparisons.
6.2 Payments and Transfers
A future payment system could support transfers between individuals, businesses, and institutions.
It would require reliable settlement procedures, user authentication, transaction records, and dispute resolution.
6.3 Deposits and Withdrawals
Deposit and withdrawal functions may be considered in a future regulated financial structure.
Such services must not be offered without appropriate authorization, safeguards, and clear information about the legal nature of user balances.
6.4 Lending and Interest
SATJA may explore lending arrangements with agreed interest rates.
The framework should disclose:
Principal amount.
Interest rate.
Repayment schedule.
Total repayment amount.
Collateral, if any.
Default conditions.
Borrower protections.
Lender responsibilities.
Interest must be governed by transparent contracts and applicable law.
6.5 Investment
Future investment services may allow participation in productive activities or eligible assets.
Investment products must clearly explain risk, ownership, liquidity, fees, and possible losses.
6.6 Separation of Functions
The system should distinguish between:
The SJT unit of account.
A payment instrument.
Deposits and user balances.
Reserve assets.
Collateral.
Credit arrangements.
Redemption rights.
Combining these concepts without clear definitions could create serious financial and legal risks.
7. Issuance, Reserves, and Liquidity
One of the most important research questions is how any future SATJA-denominated payment instrument would be issued.
Possible models include:
Issuance by a regulated institution.
Issuance against verified reserves.
A limited pilot using clearly defined settlement arrangements.
A multi-institution model with transparent reserve reporting.
Each model has different implications for trust, liquidity, stability, and governance.
7.1 Reserve Requirements
If an instrument promises redemption, the reserve policy must specify:
Eligible reserve assets.
Ownership and custody.
Independent audits.
Liquidity requirements.
Valuation procedures.
Redemption conditions.
Loss allocation.
Emergency procedures.
7.2 Liquidity
A reference index does not create a liquid market by itself.
A functioning market requires willing buyers and sellers, reliable settlement, adequate market infrastructure, and confidence in the rules.
7.3 Financial Stability
The design must be tested against:
Sudden changes in resource prices.
Bank or custodian failure.
Large redemption requests.
Market disruptions.
Fraud and cyberattacks.
Concentration of reserve assets.
No financial stability guarantee should be claimed before empirical testing.
8. Technology and Security
SATJA may use modern digital infrastructure to improve transparency, traceability, and operational security.
8.1 Possible Technology Components
Distributed ledger technology.
Conventional databases.
Hybrid database and blockchain architecture.
Digital identity systems.
Cryptographic signatures.
Secure APIs.
Independent audit interfaces.
Automated monitoring.
Backup and recovery systems.
Blockchain may be useful for tamper-evident transaction records, but it is not automatically necessary for every component.
The project should compare different architectures based on cost, performance, privacy, security, governance, and legal requirements.
8.2 Security Requirements
Potential security controls include:
Multi-factor authentication.
Encryption in transit and at rest.
Digital signatures.
Role-based access control.
Hardware security where appropriate.
Transaction monitoring.
Fraud detection.
Rate limits and withdrawal controls.
Independent security audits.
Disaster recovery and backups.
8.3 Privacy
Transparency must not mean exposing all personal information.
The system should disclose relevant rules and aggregate information while protecting private user data in accordance with applicable data protection laws.
8.4 Off-Chain Evidence
Physical assets and legal documents exist outside digital ledgers.
Therefore, SATJA must establish reliable connections between digital records and independently verifiable real-world evidence.
9. Governance and Accountability
A global monetary reference system requires credible governance.
No single individual, company, or institution should be able to change essential rules without transparent procedures and appropriate oversight.
Potential governance principles include:
Public documentation of core rules.
Independent review of valuation methods.
Transparent changes to reference basket weights.
Conflict-of-interest disclosure.
Independent financial and technical audits.
Public reporting of important system statistics.
Clear complaint and dispute-resolution procedures.
Participation by different countries and stakeholder groups.
A future governance body could include representatives from economics, finance, agriculture, technology, law, consumer protection, and independent auditing.
Its legal structure and decision-making powers would need to be studied before implementation.

10.1 Avoiding False Certainty
A resource-based reference does not guarantee that purchasing power will never decline.
Resource prices can change because of weather, technology, supply, demand, productivity, transport costs, legal changes, and other factors.
Likewise, national currencies can lose purchasing power through inflation, but a resource index can also fluctuate.
SATJA should therefore be assessed through measurable performance indicators and comparison with existing monetary benchmarks.
11. Research Questions and Evaluation Criteria
The following questions should guide independent research:
Which resources should be included in the reference basket?
How should resource categories be weighted?
How frequently should prices and quantities be updated?
How should international price differences be treated?
How can the system prevent manipulation and inaccurate data?
What legal structure would be suitable for a shared international unit of account?
What reserve and liquidity arrangements would be necessary for any payment instrument?
How could SATJA interact with existing national currencies?
What are the expected operating costs?
How does SATJA perform during inflation, recession, commodity shocks, and financial crises?
11.1 Suggested Evaluation Indicators
Researchers could evaluate:
Reference-index volatility.
Correlation with consumer price indices.
Purchasing-power stability.
Geographic coverage.
Data quality and update speed.
Market liquidity.
Transaction costs.
Operational reliability.
Auditability.
User accessibility.
Resistance to manipulation.
The results should be published, including negative or inconclusive findings.
12. Development Roadmap
SATJA should be developed in stages.
Phase 1: Concept and Documentation
Publish the proposal.
Define terminology.
Invite independent criticism.
Identify unresolved economic and legal questions.
Phase 2: Resource Data Research
Identify potential reference resources.
Collect historical price data.
Study quality standards.
Compare regional markets.
Assess data reliability.
Phase 3: Reference Index Prototype
Develop a test reference basket.
Compare alternative weighting methods.
Simulate historical performance.
Publish methodology and limitations.
Phase 4: Resource Registry Prototype
Build a demonstration registry.
Test resource identification.
Test evidence and verification procedures.
Develop duplicate-registration controls.
Phase 5: Independent Review
Request economic analysis.
Request legal analysis.
Request cybersecurity review.
Invite agricultural and industrial experts.
Publish findings and revisions.
Phase 6: Limited Pilot
A pilot may be considered only after appropriate legal review, technical testing, governance arrangements, and risk controls.
It should have limited scope, clear user protections, and no unsupported claims of guaranteed value.
Phase 7: Deployment Decision
A broader deployment decision should depend on independent evidence, legal authorization, operational readiness, and demonstrated public benefit.

15. Conclusion
SATJA proposes a global resource-referenced monetary and exchange framework built around a shared unit of account and transparent information about real-world resources.
Its central principle is that economic value should be examined through observable and verifiable economic conditions rather than depending exclusively on the monetary value of a single national currency.
The proposal seeks to combine resource information, voluntary market pricing, transparent valuation, digital infrastructure, and accountable governance.
However, a resource-referenced unit is not automatically a fully backed currency, a guaranteed store of value, or a universally redeemable financial instrument.
Those outcomes would require additional economic research, legal foundations, reserve arrangements, liquidity mechanisms, independent verification, and practical testing.
SATJA is therefore offered as an open proposal for international discussion and collaborative research.
The goal is not simply to create another form of money. The goal is to investigate whether a more transparent and resource-informed foundation for international economic exchange can be developed.
